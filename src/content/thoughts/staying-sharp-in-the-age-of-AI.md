I'm currently taking a career break after a 5-year long grind of working full time as a software engineer.
I simply lost the passion for the company I was working at-- and the lack of motivation slowly kept growing until the inevitable burnout hit me with full force.

The last 6 months I was working as employee #1 (under the co-founders) for an AI-native workspace for enterprises, called spring.new.
Before Spring, I was working at a very cushioned position as a full stack developer at an ecommerce platform called Cymbio, for around 2 years. I did lots of great things at Cymbio, designed many business critical features that are now selling points of the company, and all-in-all gained lots of invaluable professional experience from working there.
In addition to the professional aspects Cymbio brought me, it also provided me with a beautiful work-life balance that is simply hard to come by nowadays. Comfortable hybrid work model-- 2 days from home, 3 days in the office, and never expected to work past 18/19 on the weekdays, and NEVER on the weekends. With that being said though, there were many times I stayed up past midnight helping fix breaking integrations and critical issues, but it never felt forced. There was an incredible sense of family from the other engineers whenever there was a production incident.
The reason I eventually left Cymbio was due to the following reasons:

1. I increasingly grew more and more detached from the company culture, and started really dreading coming to the office. While some of the engineers and other employees are truly great people, I was getting major 'NPC' vibes from everyone in the office. Mainly feeling out-of-place, and having absolutely nothing in common with anyone working there. I usually take great pride in myself being able to be friends with anyone, even if we come from different backgrounds and have different hobbies, but the sheer personality differences I was getting from everyone started to just get too much to handle.

2. I did not think our technology was innovative or cutting-edge in the slightest. As a developer constantly building side projects with modern tools, jumping into a legacy Node.js express system with commonJS just does not feel right. I was in a crossroads and kind of in a difficult position. I had lots of strong opinions and very constructive thoughts on ways I thought the engineering team could grow, but I never felt comfortable sharing them with the R&D team. The reasons for this were the following:

- It was very difficult for me to communicate many members of the management-- mostly due to my poor Hebrew speaking abilities. Living in Israel, this is to be expected-- most of the engineers being native Hebrew speakers without the same fluence in English. There were many times where our language gaps were too strong that prevented me from being able to have a truly producive conversation.
- I was strongly perceived as a junior, and there were many times where I would be on a feature, and ask to use a specific technology-- and immediately would be shot down from the 'senior' engineers due to them not being familiar with it.

I have so many more reasons for my eventual leave, but these were the main ones.
When I gave my resignation notice to the Director of Enginnering, he interrupted his work day to meet me at a cafe in my neighborhood, and we had a good and honest discussion. He offered me other opportunities and ares to work at the company to retain me and keep my interest, i.e. joining a new AI team led by a previous founder who made 2 succesful startup exits (to Snap inc, and Ebay).
It was an extremely generous offering, and I took a few days to decide on it-- ultimately deciding not to continue with Cymbio.

I was looking for a change-- and I found that at the next place I found myself working at (spring).

I came to find spring during 1 month I had off at Cymbio. Without getting too deep with it, I was required to draft in the IDF when I was 25, and during my process of eventually getting an official release, Cymbio let me take time off. I was at a cafe on one of these days, where I met the founder of Spring. We talked, and he posed the idea of me joining to work with him. At the time, all the stars seemed to align and point me towards joining him and his company.

- a cutting edge AI startup where I will be the first-employee? Sick
- Being able to take full ownership on features and parts of the company? Check
- Using latest and greatest developer tools? Check

I imagined it would be chill and more aligned with how I work, due to how unstructured everything was. I imagined I would be able to work independently without being micromanaged, push great features, and just have a great time with the dreams of making millions with the founders, helping them reach their dream, while also being able to still enjoy my life and time as a 26 year old in tel aviv.

The day i met him at the cafe, i came in person for a technical interview, and after about 30 minutes both him and the CTO made up their minds that they want me to join. It all seemed great!

The only _slight_ problems i noticed before signing were the following:

- The salary. He offered me around 5,000 NIS less than what I was currently making. I told him, and he raised it to be only slightly above what i was making at Cymbio. Fine. okay.
- The pressure he put on me to sign. It's only the nature of the business to put pressure on applicants, but I would be lying if I said it wasn't a bit intense. "Ryan, we have many other applicants that are eager to work with us, but we are holding them for you. You have to give us an answer by the end of the day or else". At the time, Cymbio had no idea that I had this job lined up. I told Spring my whole sitation, that I would need at least a month to leave Cymbio on good terms. In the end, i signed spring, and gave Cymbio my letter of resignation.

I would not have any time off in between jobs, but that was okay for me. The time-off I had during my situation with IDF was enough time-off I needed, and I was ready to jump back into a technical role.

Little did I know that this would be one of the poorest decisions I made in my professional career.

Remember earlier, how I was excited to work for a cutting edge AI startup, with the ideas of fun and exciting challenges?
All of the excitement was tarnished within the first 2 weeks I was there.

The first few days of Spring, and I'm slowly getting familiar with the codebase, not really touching any major features yet, but actually pushing things to production! I am pretty humbled with how quickly I was able to get onboarded to such a complex system, and genuinely question how most other engineers with my amount of experience would fair if they were to be dropped in my shoes.

They had a mandatory office-every day policy, which was fine for me. I show up every day around 9:30-10, and work HARD, closely working with the CTO, and starting to push 1,000+ line PR's within my first week. I am however, leaving every day around 19:00 / 20:00, to continue living my life. Keep in mind this is around 10 hours of being inside of the office. I always make sure to align with the CTO and CEO of my progress, what I'm currently working on, and always feel like we are on the same page. Also, I always make it clear that I'm always available and close to my computer if anything breaks and they need me to hop on a call in the middle of the night. I make it so clear that my intentions are for the company to succeed, and I'll do the best I can to make it happen.

So i'm working for a few weeks at this point, and in my mind, I feel like everything is going well. One day, my CEO pulls me into a private room for a 1-on-1 meeting. He says to me (paraphrased), "Ryan, I feel like there is a cultural gap between you and me. I understand that you have your life, your passions, your band, and enjoy exercising, but we're building a spaceship here at Spring, and having you leave the office everyday at 19:00 while me and the CTO are still at the office working-- it doesn't feel right."

Immediately after hearing this, I think to myself **there is no way you are being serious**. I try to have a mature discussion about it though. I asked if it was due to my output velocity not as strong as he wanted, or if there were specific features he wanted to go live sooner-- because why else would you want someone to stay in the office later?

He replied by saying "No, I just want you to be a part of our culture at Spring. I spent time studying start-ups in San Francisco, and it's common for the developers there to eat sleep and live in the office".

I'm thinking to myself _what the actual fuck is happening_. I try to explain that in order for me to push as hard as I can and to continue having a great velocity, **I need** my routine.

We ultimately don't reach a common ground, he just wanted to tell me how he felt about me from observing me for the last few weeks, and we left it at that.

In my head, I'm thinking _what did I sign myself up for_.

For me, I will never be quick to quit just because something is difficult. I understand that they are probably under a lot of pressure, and that they want me to be close with them for late hours to feel like we are all in this together. I understand where he's coming from, _I really do._. But for me, I need my routine. For the next few weeks, I try hard to show them that I'm committed, and to try to find a perfect middle ground where I can push hard, without sacrificing everything I need that keeps me sane, i.e. my workout routine, my bands.

So things are going well, until something happens that _really_ makes me think twice about continuing my career here.

So I'm in my metalcore band, and we have an upcoming show on Thursday in Jerusalem. Our show is at 8pm, but our soundcheck is at 6pm (anyone who has ever played a gig knows how important it is to be on time for the soundcheck).
I tell the guys at Spring **weeks** in advance about the show, and that I will have to leave the office at a maximum of 4:30pm to not be late. They tell me it's not a problem.
Thursday comes around, and I bring my guitar and guitar pedalboard to the office, and I work consistantly up until the final second--4:30. I see i have a train that leaves in 15 minutes-- and it's a 15 minute walk to the train station. I tell the guys goodbye, they tell me "Good luck", and i take my guitar, pedalboard, and backpack and rush to the train.

It's the middle of July, and I am sweating in the heat.

Keep in mind that as much as I am a full-time worker, I have a full-time personal life. I wake up, cook, clean my apartment, come to work and **work hard** for 10 hours, leave the office usually immediately going to my band practice and coming home most at 21:00, or going straight to workout.
